## 1. Executive Summary

This project evaluates the financial viability of installing a residential battery storage system to complement an existing solar photovoltaic (PV) installation.

The analysis uses hourly solar electricity generation and household electricity consumption data for the 2020 calendar year to model how excess solar electricity could be stored and subsequently used when solar generation is insufficient.

The model compares two operating arrangements:

1. **Existing solar system without battery storage:** Solar generation is used immediately to meet household electricity demand. Any shortfall is purchased from the electricity provider, while excess solar generation provides no additional benefit.
2. **Solar system with battery storage:** Solar generation first meets household demand. Excess generation charges the battery, subject to its maximum storage capacity. Stored energy is subsequently used when solar generation cannot meet household demand.

The resulting hourly energy calculations are used to estimate the potential electricity cost savings associated with battery installation.

The analysis then evaluates the investment over a 20-year period under two electricity price inflation scenarios. The investment is assessed using Net Present Value (NPV) at a 6% discount rate and Internal Rate of Return (IRR).

The project combines data quality assessment, exploratory data analysis, energy modelling, battery simulation and financial investment appraisal in a reproducible Python Jupyter Notebook.

## 2. Business Background and Problem Statement

Households meet their electricity requirements through electricity purchased from an electricity provider or electricity generated by installed solar panels.

In the case considered in this assessment, Naomi has installed solar panels on the roof of her house. Electricity generated by the panels is used immediately to meet household consumption.

However, solar generation and household electricity consumption do not necessarily occur at the same time.

During periods when solar generation exceeds household demand, the excess electricity is not currently stored or converted into income. During periods when solar generation is insufficient, the household purchases the electricity shortfall from its electricity provider.

Naomi is therefore considering purchasing a battery storage system to retain excess solar generation for use at a later time.

The proposed battery investment costs $7,000 and has an expected working lifetime of 20 years.

The central business problem is:

**Would installing a battery storage system generate sufficient electricity cost savings over its expected working life to justify the initial investment?**

The assessment requires this question to be evaluated through hourly energy modelling and long-term financial projections.

## 5. Analytical Methodology

The modelling process follows the sequence specified in the assessment.

### 5.1 Hourly Electricity Purchased Without Battery Storage

Under the existing solar arrangement, household consumption is first met using electricity generated by the solar panels.

Any remaining electricity requirement is purchased from the electricity provider.

The hourly calculation is:

**Grid Electricity Purchased = MAX(Electricity Usage − Solar Generation, 0)**

This establishes the baseline grid electricity requirement without battery storage.

### 5.2 Excess Solar Electricity Generation

Excess solar electricity represents the amount of electricity generated beyond the household's immediate consumption.

The calculation is:

**Excess Solar Electricity = MAX(Solar Generation − Electricity Usage, 0)**

This excess generation represents the electricity potentially available to charge the proposed battery.

### 5.3 Battery Storage Simulation

The assessment specifies a battery with:

- Maximum storage capacity: 12.5 kWh.
- Minimum storage level: 0 kWh.
- Initial charge level: 0 kWh at 1 January 2020, 00:00.

The battery simulation tracks the cumulative storage level for every hour throughout 2020.

The battery charge increases when excess solar electricity is available and decreases when stored electricity is needed to meet household consumption.

The maximum permitted charge level is 12.5 kWh. Any excess generation when the battery is full cannot be stored.

The battery charge calculation must therefore respect both the minimum and maximum storage constraints.

### 5.4 Hourly Electricity Purchased With Battery Storage

With battery storage, household electricity demand is met in the following order:

1. Current solar electricity generation.
2. Electricity stored in the battery.
3. Electricity purchased from the electricity provider.

The remaining electricity requirement after using available solar and stored battery energy represents the grid electricity purchased.

The resulting hourly grid purchases are compared with the baseline model without battery storage.

### 5.5 Annual Electricity Cost Savings

The model calculates the difference between electricity purchased without battery storage and electricity purchased with battery storage.

This difference represents the additional electricity demand met through stored solar energy.

The assessment requires the resulting 2020 electricity savings to be valued using the electricity price effective from 1 January 2022.

The calculation ignores discounting for this historical-year savings exercise.

### 5.6 Monthly Energy Analysis

The model also tabulates and visualises monthly energy quantities for the calendar year.

The required monthly measures are:

- Total solar electricity generation.
- Total household electricity consumption.
- Electricity purchased from the provider without battery storage.
- Electricity purchased from the provider with battery storage.

The monthly comparison illustrates the relationship between solar generation, household demand and the effect of battery storage on grid electricity purchases.

## 6. Long-Term Financial Modelling

The financial evaluation projects the annual savings from battery installation over 20 years from 1 January 2022.

The two electricity price inflation scenarios are modelled separately so that the user does not need to change model parameters to view the alternative results.

### 6.1 Investment Assumptions

| Parameter | Assumption |
|---|---:|
| Initial battery investment | $7,000 |
| Installation date | 1 January 2022 |
| Expected working lifetime | 20 years |
| Maximum battery capacity | 12.5 kWh |
| Initial electricity price | $0.17 per kWh |
| Initial electricity inflation | 4% per annum |
| Discount rate | 6% per annum |
| Historical energy analysis | Calendar year 2020 |

### 6.2 Scenario 1: Constant Electricity Price Inflation

Under Scenario 1, electricity prices increase by 4% per annum.

The electricity price begins at $0.17 per kWh on 1 January 2022.

The 4% annual increase applies from 1 January 2023 and each subsequent 1 January throughout the projection period.

The model uses this price path to estimate annual electricity cost savings over the battery's 20-year working life.

### 6.3 Scenario 2: Increasing Electricity Price Inflation

Under Scenario 2, electricity price inflation starts at 4% per annum and increases by an additional 0.25 percentage points each year.

The specified inflation sequence is:

| Year | Annual Electricity Price Inflation |
|---|---:|
| 2023 | 4.00% |
| 2024 | 4.25% |
| 2025 | 4.50% |
| 2026 | 4.75% |
| 2027 | 5.00% |

The same annual progression continues throughout the projection period.

This scenario represents Naomi's expectation that electricity prices may increase faster than the government's estimated constant 4% annual inflation rate.

### 6.4 Net Present Value (NPV)

The Net Present Value measures the present value of the projected investment savings after accounting for the initial battery investment.

The assessment specifies a discount rate of 6% per annum.

The general calculation is:

**NPV = Present Value of Future Annual Savings − Initial Investment**

A positive NPV indicates that the discounted future savings exceed the initial investment under the model assumptions.

A negative NPV indicates that the discounted savings do not recover the initial investment.

### 6.5 Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

The Internal Rate of Return is the discount rate at which the present value of the future investment savings equals the initial battery investment.

The IRR is calculated independently for both electricity price inflation scenarios.

This provides another measure for assessing the investment's financial attractiveness.

## 8. Model Checks and Reasonableness Assessment

The assessment emphasises the importance of self-checks and reasonableness checks at every stage of the model.

The following areas are important when reviewing the calculations:

### Data Checks
- Expected hourly observation count.
- Missing values and time sequence.
- Significant outliers and documented corrections.

### Energy Calculation Checks
- Grid purchases should not fall below zero.
- Excess solar electricity should not fall below zero.
- Battery charge should never exceed 12.5 kWh or fall below zero.
- Battery discharge should be supported by available stored energy.
- Grid purchases with battery storage should be consistent with the remaining unmet electricity demand.

### Financial Checks
- Electricity prices should follow the selected inflation scenario.
- The initial investment should be recognised in the investment cash flows.
- Future savings should be discounted at the specified rate for NPV.
- The IRR should be consistent with the investment cash flows.
- Both scenarios should be calculated independently.
